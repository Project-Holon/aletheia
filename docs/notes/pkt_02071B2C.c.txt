It is not a constructor: it is the SLOT ALLOCATOR that owns the six matrix
tables the constructors write into.  Shape, from the listing:

  - Two slot indices arrive as bytes, `ldrb r4, [r2, #1]` and
    `ldrb r0, [r2, #2]` at 0x02071648/0x0207164C, i.e. the second argument is
    a small byte record, not a word.
  - The allocator state is one POINTER at 0x02148DB0 whose target carries a
    bitmask array at +0xC4, indexed `slot >> 5` with bit `1u << (slot & 0x1f)`.
    All three of `tst`, `orr` (claim) and `mvn`+`and` (release) appear.
  - Six 0x18-byte-stride tables at 0x02149BB4..0x02149BC8, read at one slot's
    index and written at the other's, six unrolled `smull ... >> 12` blocks
    at 0x02071784..0x02071830.  The `mul ip, r0, #0x18` / `mul r3, r4, #0x18`
    pair is what fixes the stride.
  - Four calls to func_02082A90 (0x18-byte block copy, the count is always a
    literal `mov r2, #0x18`).
  - Flag word bit 2 selects the whole upper path; the exits all `orr #0x18`
    into the object's flags at +0x00 before returning.

Four exits, three of them `pop {r4, r5, r6, r7, r8, pc}`, so the body is a
four-way dispatch and not a loop.  The seven trailing pool words at
0x0207183C..0x02071854 are the eight data addresses above.

The cheap part first, for whoever takes it: the bitmask claim/release helper
is a two-instruction idiom repeated four times and is almost certainly a
static inline in the original; getting that shape right is likely to settle
most of the register allocation in one go.
